How a recurrent nova works

A cool giant and a dead star, locked close enough that one feeds the other until the fuel ignites.

Red giant donor (cool M4 III star) [1][2] White dwarf accretor (hot, compact)

Eruption timeline

Two confirmed eruptions, about 80 years apart. Historical records also suggest a highly probable one in 1787 and a more uncertain one in 1217. [2]

1866
First well-observed nova. Discovered at about magnitude 2, only 2.8 hours after it was last seen fainter than magnitude 5 to 6. [1]
1946
Second eruption, about 80 years later. Its peak was missed by about half a day, but the light curve matches 1866's, so the peak was also about magnitude 2. [1][2]
From 2015
Entered a high state, as it did before 1946. [1]
Now
80 years since 1946. No one can give an exact date.

When is it difficult to see or photograph?

T CrB is never lost in the Sun's glare, but for some weeks each autumn it only sits low in the sky when the sky is truly dark. Black is easy; blue is difficult. Shown for latitude 36° N across one year.

Finding it in the sky

Corona Borealis, the "Northern Crown" — a small, distinctive semicircle of stars between Boötes and Hercules. T CrB sits just off the arc, too faint to see normally. Aaron's own capture, Seestar S50, 2024-08-24.
