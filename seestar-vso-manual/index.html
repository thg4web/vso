Where we add our own measurements and steps (S30 Pro, T CrB, October 2026), they are labelled as ours, are provisional, and are covered in more detail in the provisional T CrB photometry guide. Where sources disagree, both sides are here rather than picked for you. Nothing below is invented; if a step isn’t sourced yet, it isn’t in here yet.

Is this realistic?

Yes, with real limits worth knowing up front:

  • Precision: beginner-level work with careful technique lands around 0.05–0.1 mag agreement against other observers (arnaudfiocret, AAVSO forum, Sep 2024, using ASTAP + the green channel). That’s good enough to track real variability in stars with an amplitude of a few tenths of a magnitude or more — not good enough to chase millimagnitude transits.
  • Saturation: the Seestar’s sensor saturates around V ≈ 8. Linear and well-behaved below that (cross-checked against Gaia DR2 by a Cloudy Nights user). On our S30 Pro frames the red and blue pixels reach 65535 on bright stars, while the green pixels top out near 48,000 to 49,000, a soft ceiling below 65535. It depends on the channel, exposure, gain and model, so check the pixel values in your own frames. Above it — don’t trust the numbers. Independently reported by multiple people (see the SUNY Dutchess/MHAA project below) — this is a real, recurring limit, not a one-off.
  • Field rotation used to be a real problem for long time-series runs in the Seestar’s native alt-az mode. Andrew Pearce (AAVSO forum, Aug 2025) calls EQ mode “a key enabler” that removed this as an issue entirely.

It’s already been done, on T CrB specifically

Bikeman (British Astronomical Association, Variable Star Section — BAA-VSS), AAVSO forum, April 12, 2024: picked T CrB as a target specifically because it was sitting around V≈10 at the time — “a sweet spot for the S50… not too bright, not too faint” — and noted the ongoing campaign watching for her next eruption. By averaging 10×10s subs, got down to a standard deviation of about 0.01 mag in TG. Results are in the AAVSO light curve under observer code EHEA, date 2024-04-10.

That’s direct precedent: someone already ran this exact playbook, on this exact star, with this exact scope, and it worked. Worth pulling up EHEA’s AAVSO submissions as a reference point for what a clean run looks like.

A second, ongoing real project: Leonides Lopez and Maria Garcia Martinez, students at SUNY Dutchess, presented a status report to the Mid-Hudson Astronomical Association (MHAA) in April 2026 — the fifth in a line of MHAA student experiments specifically testing whether a Seestar S50 is viable for at-home variable star research. They’ve been tracking T CrB continuously since June 20, 2025 (a baseline started by their advisor, Dr. Myers), alongside Polaris and a couple of other targets, using AstroImageJ. Source: “Variable Star Photometry & T CrB Nova Watch with the Seestar S50”, MHAA YouTube, Sep 2026. (It’s an auto-captioned transcript — the numbers and names below came through clearly, a couple of other star names did not and aren’t repeated here.)

What they found, worth knowing before you start:

  • Checked their AstroImageJ-derived T CrB magnitudes against AAVSO’s published community data over their observing baseline: average percent error 0.845%, best case 0.15%, worst case 1.65% on one specific day. Independent confirmation this approach gets you genuinely close to the crowd-sourced consensus.
  • Hit the same saturation problem independently — AstroImageJ flagged “oversaturated” stars on some of their stacks (sometimes a soft yellow warning, sometimes a hard error), and they hadn’t fully root-caused whether it was an AstroImageJ setting or a genuine Seestar limit by the time of this talk. Their advisor separately found Vega itself “too darn bright” to use and had to pick dimmer stars nearby instead. Three independent reports of saturation trouble now (this one, Bikeman’s V≈8 ceiling, and the general forum discussion) — treat it as a real, recurring limit, not a fluke.
  • Pointing drift fix: when the Seestar wouldn’t stay pointed at their actual target, they’d manually resync on Polaris first (fixed, never moves, so the scope “knows exactly where it is” again) before re-slewing to the variable star.
  • They sourced comparison-star magnitudes from both Stellarium and AAVSO’s own comparison-star sequence charts — two independent cross-checks rather than trusting one source blind.
  • When graphing in Excel, they reverse the Y-axis specifically to match AAVSO’s own light-curve convention (brighter = up) — exactly the same choice this project’s own T CrB chart already makes.
  • They explicitly excluded AAVSO’s “Fainter-than” flagged points from their comparison data, since those are upper-limit non-detections that don’t represent a real measured brightness — also exactly how this project’s own data pipeline already treats fainter_than observations.
  • One fact worth folding into the T CrB page itself at some point: in the Q&A, they cited T CrB’s orbital period as roughly 227 days — a completely different number from, and not to be confused with, her ~80-year recurrent-nova eruption cycle. This checks out: Schaefer (2023, MNRAS 524, 3146) gives an orbital period of 227.5687 ± 0.0099 days.
  • Honest null result: they also tried Polaris as a target and got nothing useful — its real variability range (~0.07 mag) is smaller than their measurement precision can resolve. Their own words: “not variable enough.” Good reminder that not every target is a fit for this gear, and that’s a legitimate finding, not a failure.

The app’s Photometry mode (v3.4.x): what it is and is not

The Seestar app gained a Photometry mode in v3.4.0 (October 2026). It writes a CSV per frame and a summary CSV with the star’s position, Mag(obs), Mag(err) and SNR. What we found on the S30 Pro:

  • Its magnitude is not V, and it reads about 0.9 magnitude too bright for every star we checked (a roughly constant zero-point error). It read about 9.2 for T CrB where AAVSO observers reported about 10.0. Which band and catalogue it uses is not documented.
  • Mag(err) is not photon noise. It is mostly the scatter of the app’s own reference stars, and one clipped bright reference can dominate it, so the app’s magnitude depends on which references it happens to use. Do not quote it as the measurement error.
  • ZWO has not published how it works. The v3.4.0 and v3.4.1 release notes say only “Added Photometry” and that the dark-frame library workflow changed. Whether the saved frames are dark-subtracted is unknown.
  • Use it to identify the star and to get pixel positions, not for the magnitude. The X Axis and Y Axis columns are 1-based pixel positions in the raw frame (we verified that the pixel peaks sit at X−1, Y−1).
  • It can record several stars at once. Select more than one star and the app writes a row for each in every frame’s CSV, plus a summary CSV per star. It may not keep every star in every frame.

Our Seestar photometry steps (provisional)

These steps are ours, from two test nights on one telescope, and they are early. We tested them on a Seestar S30 Pro (telephoto camera, IRCUT filter, equatorial mode, gain 200, app v3.4.0) on the red recurrent nova T CrB. They should carry over to other targets and to the S50 and S50 Pro, but we have not tried that, so check each point on your own frames. The worked numbers, and what is still unknown, are in the provisional T CrB photometry guide. We will revise this section as we learn more.

Before the session

  1. Get AAVSO’s comparison stars. Fetch the comparison sequence for your target from AAVSO’s Variable Star Plotter (VSP) and write down each star’s AUID and V magnitude. Never substitute a star you merely know. Pick comparison stars within about one magnitude of your target, not much brighter (bright stars saturate and force a long extrapolation). For T CrB (V about 10) we used chart X43296GGQ, stars V 10.17, 10.55, 10.74 and 11.17.
  2. Use equatorial (EQ) mode and keep it the same every night. Alt-az rotates the field during an exposure; we have not tested it. The FITS keyword EQMODE records the mode.
  3. Choose one exposure and one gain for the whole night, and check the FITS GAIN header afterwards. A gain set in the app beforehand did not take effect for us (the header stayed at 200), so do not assume it did. Choose them so that your target and comparison stars stay below the ceiling in the channel you will measure: 65535 for red and blue, but only about 45,000 for green, which saturates near 48,000 to 49,000 (as a starting point, a brightest pixel of 30,000 to 40,000; this is a suggestion, not tested).
  4. Record the app version (it is not in the FITS headers), the date, conditions (seeing, transparency, Moon), exposure, gain and mount mode.

At the telescope

  1. Take at least 5 individual sub-frames; 15 or more is better so you can see the scatter. Keep every sub: the stack hides per-frame problems.
  2. Save a short, non-enhanced stack at the end. You need it to place stars on the frames. Do not use an AI-enhanced image for measuring: it is stretched and denoised, so it is no longer linear.

Measuring

  1. Work on the raw FITS (16-bit, BITPIX 16, BZERO 32768). Do not debayer. Read the BAYERPAT header: our S30 Pro frames read GRBG (checked against the star signals); the community reports the original S50 as BGGR, so check yours, and do not crop before you separate the colour planes.
  2. Measure the green pixels only. On our frames the red and blue pixels reached 65535 on bright stars while the green pixels topped out near 48,000 to 49,000, so green gives more room than red or blue, but it still saturates below 65535. Place the target and each comparison star on every frame (we fit the app’s per-frame CSV positions to RA and Dec, then re-centre each star with a centroid), then do aperture photometry: radius 5 pixels, sky as the median of an annulus from 9 to 14 pixels, flux as the sum of (pixel − sky). The S30 Pro’s plate scale is about 3.7 arcseconds per pixel (2.9 µm pixels, 160 mm focal length, 3.67 measured).
  3. Reject saturated stars. In the red and blue planes treat any pixel at 65000 or more as clipped; in the green plane treat about 45,000 or more as saturated, because the green peaks of stars of very different brightness pile up at about 48,000 to 49,000 (a soft ceiling). Apply this to the target and every comparison star. If a star saturates, drop it or shorten the exposure.
  4. Calibrate each frame. The zero point is the median of (catalogue V − instrumental magnitude) over your comparison stars. Then check it: treat each comparison star in turn as the unknown and see whether you recover its catalogue value (we got within about 0.02 to 0.05 magnitude). A star that does not recover its value means the night is not usable. Confirm the zero point is steady across frames and that flux scales with exposure time (60 s against 30 s gave a ratio of about 2.0).
  5. Combine the frames and report the mean with its scatter.

The colour problem

  1. A red star measured against bluer comparison stars reads too bright. T CrB is far redder than any AAVSO comparison star in its field, and the green channel measured against V comparison stars read too bright. If your target is much redder (or bluer) than your comparison stars, correct for colour. Our method: use Gaia DR3 stars in the same field, find how the instrumental green magnitude relates to Gaia colour (BP−RP), apply it to the target’s Gaia colour, and convert Gaia G to V with the relation in the Gaia documentation (we checked it against AAVSO’s V for eight comparison stars: root-mean-square 0.025). Expect a systematic uncertainty of about ±0.2 magnitude for a very red star: the red end is the weak spot, because very few calibration stars are as red as T CrB. The guide has our early numbers.

Reporting

  1. Call it TG. A green-channel measurement calibrated against V comparison stars, without a transformation, is reported to AAVSO as TG, not V (see “What to call your band” below). Read the current AAVSO pages before your first submission. We have not submitted anything yet.

Quality checklist before you trust a night: no saturated pixels in the target or any comparison star, in the measured channel (for green, no peak above about 45,000); at least one comparison star recovered within its expected error; a steady zero point; at least 5 frames; your colour correction stated with its uncertainty; and your location removed from anything you share. FITS headers carry your observing latitude and longitude, and some JPGs carry GPS and an owner field with the same.

What to call your band (important — don’t mislabel this)

The Seestar’s native R/G/B channels, reported untransformed, are AAVSO’s TR / TG / TB bands (Tri-Color Red / Green / Blue — these are real AAVSO band codes, not a Seestar invention).

MZK’s rule (AAVSO forum, Sep 2024): “If your camera image has a TG bayer filter channel, report your magnitude as TG but use the known V comparison star magnitudes for untransformed photometry.” — i.e. it’s fine to measure against V-magnitude comp stars without deriving a full transformation, as long as you’re honest that your result is TG, not true V.

If you want closer-to-standard V/B/R, apply AAVSO’s own BVR transformation coefficients for the Seestar S50 (S50 only: they do not carry over to the S30 Pro or S50 Pro without checking) (official page, plus a community-derived set posted by Andrew Pearce with R² near 0.99 from the NGC 3532 standard field — worth having both to cross-check).

Known color problems, reported directly, not smoothed over (Bikeman, AAVSO forum, Sep 2024, observing a nova in Vulpecula):

  • TR (red channel): results looked “ok-ish.”
  • TG (green channel): scattered a lot for this particular target — likely because the object was red with emission concentrated near the edge of the green filter’s band (probably H-alpha). TG is not universally reliable; it depends on your target’s color and spectrum.
  • TB (blue channel): internally consistent with other observers’ blue measurements, but “very much off” compared to true Johnson B.

Takeaway: TG is the most commonly used and generally most reliable channel, but check your specific target isn’t a case (very red, strong narrow emission near 500-560nm) where it’s known to misbehave. A very red star without strong emission lines also needs care: on two October 2026 S30 Pro nights, T CrB (a red giant) measured in the green channel against V comparison stars read brighter than its true V, by roughly 0.06 to 0.3 magnitude, until we added a Gaia colour correction. Details and caveats are in the provisional T CrB guide.

Capture technique

  • Several stars in one session. The app’s Photometry mode lets you select more than one star to measure at once. Extra stars that are fainter than the target stay below the saturation ceiling and make useful check stars.
  • Use EQ mode for anything longer than a quick check — kills field rotation, which otherwise ruins long time-series photometry (Andrew Pearce, Aug 2025).
  • Average multiple short subs per data point, don’t rely on single frames. kb0fhp (AAVSO forum, Aug 2025) uses groups of 12–18 subs at 20s each (240–360s total) per point — “lets me see a little deeper, and reduces the noise in the data,” from a Bortle 7 (light-polluted) backyard.
  • More averaging reduces scatter in the light curve itself, but doesn’t necessarily improve derived quantities like time-of-maximum in a polynomial fit — Roy Axelsen (AAVSO forum, Mar 2026) ran this as a real experiment on a Delta Scuti star and found the fit error was about the same either way, even though the averaged curve looked visually cleaner. One run, not a broad study — worth keeping in mind rather than assuming “more averaging = more precise” unconditionally.
  • Take flat frames — the native Seestar app gained this capability at some point before Aug 2025 (Andrew Pearce), along with a planning mode that lets you set up an all-night unattended run.

Automation / control software

  • seestar_run — Python batching script. Andrew Pearce used this for 10-11 hour unattended all-night runs (Jul 2024 post) and, as of Aug 2025, still calls it sufficient for his needs even after seestar_alp matured.
  • seestar_alp — broader control software. Andrew had trouble getting it working as of Sep 2024; by Aug 2025 he reports it’s become his “go to software to control the Seestar.” Software that was rough a year ago may be solid now — check current state before dismissing it.

Good first targets to practice on

Andrew Pearce’s method (Smart Telescope Underworld, Jul 2024): search AAVSO’s VSX for stars in your scope’s clean brightness range, with periods under 4-6 hours and amplitude 0.4-0.5 mag. Delta Scuti variables (short-period pulsators) fit this well — his example was EH Lib, amplitude ~0.5 mag, period ~2 hours, clean light curve from a single night’s run.

T CrB herself isn’t a good practice target by this method — her “period” is a human lifetime, not a few hours — but these short-period stars are a good way to build skill and confidence on the workflow while you wait on her.
